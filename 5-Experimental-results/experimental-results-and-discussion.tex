\subsection{Benchmark Overview}
We compare Approximate Convex Decomposition (ACD) and Visibility Clique Cover
(VCC) within an otherwise identical GCS--B\'ezier planning pipeline. The
benchmark comprises five structured map families---\code{clustered},
\code{narrows}, \code{rooms}, \code{u\_shaped}, and \code{flappy}---with ten
independently generated geometries per family. All geometries use a workspace
scale of $L=5$, dense obstacle placement, medium obstacle size, and
area-coverage generation. ACD is deterministic under the fixed configuration
and is run once per geometry. To expose the stochastic variability of the
visibility sampling and clique cover, VCC is run with ten decomposition seeds
per geometry. The resulting evaluation contains 50 ACD runs and 500 VCC trials;
all method comparisons use the geometry, rather than the VCC trial, as the
independent unit of analysis.

ACD produced a valid trajectory on all 50 geometries. VCC succeeded in 489 of
500 trials: 100/100 on \code{clustered} and \code{u\_shaped}, 98/100 on
\code{flappy}, 96/100 on \code{rooms}, and 95/100 on \code{narrows}. Every
geometry had at least one successful VCC trial, but the within-geometry success
rate fell to 0.70 on \code{narrows} seed~3 and to 0.90 on \code{narrows}
seeds~1 and~8. These outcomes show that a single successful VCC realization
does not characterize robustness in a narrow-passage environment.

\subsection{Model Size and Runtime}
The runtime results separate into three computational regimes. Visibility-based
aggregation is beneficial online when it removes both regions and
intersections, as in \code{clustered} and \code{rooms}; it is ineffective when
the resulting overlap graph remains dense, as in \code{u\_shaped}; and it is
pure overhead when both methods induce essentially the same graph, as in
\code{flappy}. Here, $N_R=|V|$ is the number of convex regions and $N_E=|E|$ is
the number of directed adjacency edges in the GCS graph.
\emph{Success} reports the number of runs producing a valid certified
trajectory over the total number of attempts; each map family therefore
contains 10 ACD attempts and 100 VCC attempts.
The quantities $t_{\rm dec}$, $t_{\rm solve}$, and
$t_{\rm e2e}=t_{\rm dec}+t_{\rm solve}$ denote the decomposition time,
the downstream GCS solution time, and the per-run end-to-end planning
time, respectively. Table~\ref{tab:exp-runtime} reports the typical scale of each
regime using medians. Table~\ref{tab:exp-runtime-var} reports sample standard
deviation (SD) and IQR separately, so stability can be assessed without
embedding three statistics in every cell. The independent unit is the map
geometry. A VCC geometry contributes the median of its ten trials; failed trials
remain in the success endpoint and are excluded only from trajectory-dependent
quantities.

The decomposition and GCS stages favor different methods. On
\code{clustered}, VCC removes approximately $82\%$ of the regions and $88\%$
of the edges, reducing the median GCS solve time from 102.63~s to 3.74~s.
However, its decomposition takes an additional 122.22~s, which is greater than
the 98.89~s saved during the GCS solve. VCC is therefore slower for a single
query. The same pattern appears on \code{rooms}: VCC saves 3.30~s in the GCS
solve but adds 16.43~s during decomposition. These results indicate that VCC is
most useful when the same decomposition can be reused for several queries.

The number of regions alone does not determine the size of the GCS problem.
On \code{u\_shaped}, VCC has six fewer regions at the median but approximately
eleven more edges, and its solve time is almost twice that of ACD. Each graph
edge adds variables and constraints to the GCS formulation; merging regions can
therefore reduce $N_R$ without reducing $N_E$. On \code{flappy}, the regions
form an almost linear sequence, and both methods have median
$(N_R,N_E)=(25,24)$. VCC does not reduce the GCS graph in this case, but it
still incurs the cost of visibility sampling and clique-cover construction.

The SD and IQR values also show how much the results change across geometries.
For \code{narrows}, the VCC edge count has SD 9.31 and IQR 14.13, compared with
1.25 and 1.75 for ACD. This larger variation occurs in the map family with the
lowest VCC success rate. For \code{flappy}, both graph-size IQRs are zero,
whereas the VCC decomposition-time IQR is 0.37~s. Thus, the VCC decomposition
time varies even when the resulting graph size remains unchanged.

The family-level medians do not show one consistent direction for GCS solve
time. VCC is faster on \code{clustered} and \code{rooms}, slower on
\code{narrows} and \code{u\_shaped}, and similar to ACD on \code{flappy}.
This variation confirms that the graph structure of each map family matters
more than the decomposition method alone. In contrast, ACD has a lower median
end-to-end time in all five families because its decomposition stage is much
shorter. ACD is therefore more suitable for a single query under the tested
setup. VCC remains useful when the decomposition can be reused for repeated
queries and its initial cost can be spread across those queries.

\subsection{Correlation Between Model Size and Solve Time}
Figure~\ref{fig:exp-correlations} relates graph size to solve time using the
current benchmark data. Each of the 100 observations represents one method and
one geometry: the ACD result or the median of ten VCC trials for that geometry.
Edge count has a stronger relationship with solve time than region count. The
Spearman coefficients are $\rho=0.884$
($p=4.34\times10^{-34}$) for $N_E$ and $\rho=0.514$
($p=4.59\times10^{-8}$) for $N_R$. Both relationships are statistically
significant, but the coefficient for edge count is much larger.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{5-Experimental-results/figures/correlation_geometry_level.pdf}
\caption{Relationship between graph size and solve time for the current benchmark ($n=100$ method--geometry observations). Each VCC observation is the median of ten trials on the same geometry. Both axes use logarithmic scales, and colors indicate the decomposition method. The coefficients and $p$-values are from two-sided Spearman rank-correlation tests.}
\label{fig:exp-correlations}
\end{figure}

This difference can be explained by the structure of the GCS optimization
problem. Each region adds state variables and constraints for that region. Each
directed edge adds a flow variable and copies of the state variables at both
ends of the edge. For a B\'ezier trajectory, these variables include control
points and timing variables. The edge also adds continuity, derivative, and
cost constraints. Therefore, increasing $N_E$ can add many variables and
constraints to the optimization problem. A dense graph also gives the solver
more possible routes to consider during presolve, relaxation, and rounding.
In comparison, $N_R$ only counts the regions and does not show how many regions
overlap. This helps explain why two decompositions with similar $N_R$ can have
very different solve times. However, the correlation does not prove that edge
count alone causes the increase in solve time, because the analysis combines
different map families and both decomposition methods.

\subsection{Trajectory Quality}
Trajectory quality shows a different comparison from runtime. Neither method
consistently produces shorter paths, but VCC generally produces higher minimum
clearance. The size of this difference depends on the map structure.
Table~\ref{tab:exp-quality} reports the median values, while
Table~\ref{tab:exp-quality-var} reports SD and IQR. Reporting them separately
helps distinguish the typical result from occasional extreme values.

The VCC stopping rule is important when interpreting this result. VCC uses 500
samples to estimate coverage. It stops when the estimated coverage reaches
0.96 or after 500 iterations. This does not mean that exactly 4\% of the free
space remains uncovered, and it does not show that the remaining area consists
of narrow passages. It is only a stopping condition based on sampled points.
However, narrow areas or areas with few samples are less likely to form large
visibility cliques and overlapping regions. This may partly explain why VCC
produces larger regions and higher clearance in some maps, while still having
connection failures in narrow passages. Therefore, coverage, connectivity, and
clearance should be evaluated as separate properties.

The path-length results differ across the map families. VCC has a longer median
path on \code{clustered} and \code{u\_shaped}, a shorter median path on
\code{narrows} and \code{rooms}, and almost the same median path as ACD on
\code{flappy}. Thus, the results do not show that either method generally
produces shorter paths. The decomposition changes the routes available to the
optimizer, and its effect depends on the map structure.

Minimum clearance has a clearer pattern. VCC has a higher median than ACD in all
five map families. The difference is large on \code{clustered} and
\code{u\_shaped}, where large visibility regions give the trajectory more room
to stay away from obstacle boundaries. The difference is small on
\code{flappy}, because the narrow corridor leaves little space for either
method to change the route.

SD and IQR show whether the variation is common across geometries or caused by
a few extreme cases. For ACD on \code{clustered}, the clearance SD is
$1.01\times10^{-1}$, while the IQR is only $1.16\times10^{-4}$. This large
difference suggests that a small number of extreme values increase the SD. For
VCC on \code{u\_shaped}, the SD is $1.97\times10^{-2}$ and the IQR is
$2.45\times10^{-2}$. In this case, the clearance varies across many of the
geometries, not only a few extreme cases. Overall, VCC improves clearance in
some map families, but it also requires more decomposition time and is less
reliable in narrow passages. Therefore, neither method is better in every
aspect.

The relaxation results lead to the same conclusion. The chain-like
\code{flappy} graphs contain few alternative routes, so the relaxed and rounded
objective values remain close. The more connected \code{clustered} graphs
contain more alternative routes, and the relaxed flow can be divided among
them. Therefore, reducing the number of regions or edges does not always reduce
the difference between the relaxed and rounded solutions. Graph size,
relaxation quality, clearance, and total runtime should be evaluated
separately.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}
The experiments identify the induced edge structure, rather than the number of
convex regions alone, as the most informative decomposition-level predictor of
GCS cost. VCC is advantageous online when visibility-based aggregation removes
both regions and adjacencies, as on \code{clustered} and \code{rooms}; it loses
that advantage when aggregation leaves a dense overlap graph, as on
\code{u\_shaped}, or leaves the graph essentially unchanged, as on
\code{flappy}. These results motivate reporting $N_E$, presolved program size,
relaxation and rounding behavior, and reliability alongside $N_R$.

The appropriate method consequently depends on query regime. For a single
start--goal query, ACD dominates end-to-end runtime in every tested family and
also achieves a 50/50 geometry success rate. VCC becomes attractive when the
environment decomposition is reusable: its one-time cost can be amortized over
multiple start--goal pairs, changes in trajectory boundary conditions, or
repeated replanning on a static map. If $t_{\rm dec}^{A}$ and
$t_{\rm dec}^{V}$ denote decomposition costs and $t_{\rm GCS}^{A}$ and
$t_{\rm GCS}^{V}$ denote per-query solve costs, VCC breaks even after
approximately
\(
Q^{\star}=(t_{\rm dec}^{V}-t_{\rm dec}^{A})/
(t_{\rm GCS}^{A}-t_{\rm GCS}^{V})
\)
queries whenever $t_{\rm GCS}^{A}>t_{\rm GCS}^{V}$. The family medians give
$Q^{\star}\approx2$ for \code{clustered} and $Q^{\star}\approx6$ for
\code{rooms}; no positive break-even point exists for families in which VCC
does not reduce per-query GCS time. This calculation assumes that the graph can
be reused without rebuilding and excludes any query-dependent preprocessing.

The clearance results expose a separate design trade-off. VCC often returns
trajectories with greater sampled minimum clearance, plausibly because larger
visibility cliques provide broader overlapping sets through which the
continuous trajectory can move. Achieving those sets requires extensive
visibility sampling and clique construction, however, and global coverage does
not ensure that every narrow gateway is represented robustly. A decomposition
selected for clearance may therefore incur both additional offline cost and
stochastic connectivity risk. Applications should treat coverage, graph
connectivity, and clearance as distinct acceptance criteria.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. First, the 50 geometries cover one
workspace scale, density level, obstacle-size level, VCC sample budget (500),
and coverage target (0.96). Second, the supplied summary CSVs retain quartiles
and population variance but omit trial-level certificate fields and failure
messages; consequently, the proposed narrow-gateway mechanism cannot be
verified without the raw logs. Third, each family contains only ten geometries;
the reported medians, SDs, and IQRs describe this benchmark but do not establish
that the same differences will hold for a broader set of maps. Finally, sampled
minimum clearance is sensitive
to trajectory discretization and is not a continuous-time collision
certificate. Future experiments should vary sampling budget, target coverage,
workspace scale, and obstacle density; retain stage-resolved failures; evaluate
graph connectivity before optimization; and measure amortized performance over
explicit multi-query workloads.
