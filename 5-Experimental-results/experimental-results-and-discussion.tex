\subsection{Benchmark Overview}
The benchmark evaluates two automatic free-space decomposition methods,
Approximate Convex Decomposition (ACD) and Visibility Clique Cover (VCC), within
the same GCS--B\'ezier motion-planning pipeline. It uses five map families:
\code{clustered}, \code{narrows}, \code{rooms}, \code{u\_shaped}, and
\code{flappy}. Each family is generated with five seeds under the same workspace
scale, obstacle density, obstacle size, and start--goal configuration. This
design yields 25 paired instances and 50 method runs.

ACD solved all 25 instances. VCC solved 23 of 25 instances; both failures occurred
on the \code{narrows} family, at seeds 2 and 3. Before termination, the failed
VCC runs reported 11 vertices and 26 edges for seed 2 and 15 vertices and 57
edges for seed 3. Graph size alone therefore does not distinguish these failures
from successful cases. The available diagnostics cannot separate the effects of
visibility sampling, coverage requirements, start--goal connectivity, and
narrow-gateway geometry.

The largest downstream reduction occurs on the \code{clustered} maps. Relative
to ACD, VCC reduces the median number of regions from 104 to 22, the number of
GCS edges from 462 to 99, and the GCS wall time from 40.10~s to 3.10~s. In this
map family, visibility-based grouping therefore produces a smaller and faster
downstream optimization problem.

The same pattern does not hold across all map families. On \code{u\_shaped},
VCC reduces the median number of regions from 25 to 16, but the median edge count
changes from 86 to 89. On \code{rooms}, one VCC run has 33 regions, 430 edges,
and a GCS wall time of 105~s. These cases illustrate why region count must be
interpreted together with the induced edge structure.

Under the tested configuration, ACD has a lower median end-to-end runtime in all
five map families. The VCC setup uses 500 samples and a target coverage of 0.96,
and its median decomposition time exceeds that of ACD in every family. Even on
\code{clustered}, where VCC reduces median GCS solve time by more than an order
of magnitude, its median decomposition time of 85.80~s results in a higher
end-to-end runtime. These measurements favor ACD for single-query planning in
this benchmark; VCC may become preferable when one decomposition is reused for
multiple queries.

\subsection{Correlation Between Model Size and Solve Time}
Figure~\ref{fig:exp-correlations} shows the relationship between model-size indicators and solve time over the 48 successful runs. The strongest Spearman correlations with \code{SolvePath} time are obtained by the presolved model size: $\rho=0.986$ for the number of presolved variables and $\rho=0.983$ for the number of presolved constraints. Among graph-level quantities available before solver construction, the number of edges is more predictive than the number of vertices, with $\rho=0.982$ compared with $\rho=0.839$.

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{5-Experimental-results/figures/correlation_panels.pdf}
\caption{Relationships between model-size indicators and solve time over 48 successful runs. Log axes are used where the metric range spans several orders of magnitude.}
\label{fig:exp-correlations}
\end{figure}

This result is consistent with the GCS formulation. Edges do not merely encode topological adjacency; they also introduce flow variables and continuity constraints between adjacent convex sets. A decomposition with fewer regions can still create a difficult optimization problem if it induces a dense graph. Consequently, the number of regions alone is an incomplete proxy for computational difficulty.

has uniformly shorter paths or traversal times. ACD has shorter median paths on
\code{clustered} and \code{u\_shaped}, whereas VCC has a shorter median path on
\code{rooms}. Their median path lengths differ by 0.10 on \code{flappy}; only
three successful VCC \code{narrows} runs are available for comparison.

VCC has a higher median minimum clearance in every map family with successful
runs. The present experiments establish this association but do not identify
which property of the visibility-based cover causes it. The clearance difference
also coincides with higher decomposition cost and two VCC failures in narrow
passages. Relaxation and rounding behavior varies by topology. On
\code{flappy}, the relaxation and rounded objectives are nearly identical,
indicating a tight relaxation for this corridor-like structure. On
\code{clustered}, VCC exhibits a larger relaxation--rounding gap; a smaller
region graph therefore does not necessarily yield a tighter rounded solution.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}
Region count alone is insufficient to characterize GCS difficulty. The results
instead support evaluating a decomposition through its induced edge count,
presolved model size, relaxation and rounding behavior, decomposition time, and
final trajectory quality. Under the tested single-query pipeline, ACD is the
more reliable and faster method. VCC may be preferable when its decomposition
can be amortized across repeated solves or when it reduces connectivity enough
to offset its higher construction cost, as observed on \code{clustered} for
GCS solve time alone.

The study has several limitations. Each map family contains only five seeds, and
VCC has only three successful \code{narrows} runs. All experiments use the same
workspace scale, obstacle density, obstacle size, and area-coverage mode. The
reported medians and correlations are descriptive; the benchmark does not report
confidence intervals or significance tests. The reported values are extracted
from diagnostic outputs, so metrics absent from those diagnostics, such as mean
clearance and smoothness proxies, are not analyzed. A broader evaluation should
vary workspace scale, obstacle density, VCC sampling budget, coverage target,
and the number of seeds.
