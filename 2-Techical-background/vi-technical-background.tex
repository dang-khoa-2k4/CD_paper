\section{Technical Background}
\label{sec:technical-background}

\subsection{Convex Free-Space Covers and Graphs of Convex Sets}
\label{sec:convex-free-space}

Obstacle avoidance is difficult because the free configuration space
$\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}}$ is generally non-convex.  The GCS--B\'ezier
approach represents the portion relevant to a planning query by a finite cover
of collision-free convex regions,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{Q}=\{Q_i\}_{i=1}^{N_R},\qquad
  Q_i\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{free}},\qquad
  N_R=|\mathcal{Q}|.
  \label{eq:convex-cover}
\end{equation}
For each query, the cover includes designated regions
$Q_\sigma,Q_\tau\in\mathcal{Q}$ such that
$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{start}}\in Q_\sigma$ and
$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{goal}}\in Q_\tau$.

The cover induces a directed Graph of Convex Sets (GCS),
$G=(V,E)$.  Each vertex $v_i\in V$ represents $Q_i$, so
$|V|=N_R$, and each directed edge represents a feasible transition between
two regions.  We write $N_E=|E|$ for the number of such transitions.  In
particular, an edge is considered only when the two regions have a nonempty
common interface and the associated trajectory-compatibility constraints can
be satisfied.  GCS then jointly chooses a route from $\sigma$ to $\tau$ and
the continuous trajectory variables in the selected regions
~\cite{MarcucciEtAl2023GCS,MarcucciEtAl2023MotionPlanning}.

Convexity is essential: every convex combination of points in $Q_i$ remains
collision free.  Moreover, a nonempty overlap or shared boundary between
neighboring regions provides a candidate connection set for consecutive
trajectory segments.  At the same time, a region that overlaps many others can
create many graph edges.  Consequently, a cover with fewer regions need not
produce a smaller or easier GCS problem; both $N_R$ and $N_E$ affect the
downstream model size.

In the GCS--B\'ezier formulation, a local trajectory is represented by
B\'ezier control points constrained to its assigned region.  The convex-hull
property of B\'ezier curves then certifies collision freedom over the whole
segment rather than only at sampled points.  The following subsections describe
the two cover-construction methods compared in this study.

\subsection{Approximate Convex Decomposition}
\label{sec:acd}

Approximate Convex Decomposition (ACD) is a geometry-driven method for
decomposing a non-convex polygon~\cite{LienAmato2006}.  In a standard
hull-based construction, concavity is measured by how far a concave boundary
feature lies from the polygon's convex hull.  ACD selects a prominent reflex
notch, inserts an interior cut
that resolves the notch, and recursively repeats this operation.  It stops when
the residual concavity of every component is below a user-selected tolerance.
Thus, a smaller tolerance retains finer geometric features and generally yields
more local pieces, whereas a larger tolerance suppresses minor features.

The cut-induced pieces meet along shared boundary portions.  When these
interfaces admit the GCS compatibility constraints, they become edges in the
region graph.  Therefore, resolving fine features can create numerous local
regions and shared interfaces, increasing $N_R$ and often $N_E$.  ACD is a
useful low-cost baseline for testing whether a fast cover-construction stage
offsets the optimization cost of a larger downstream graph.

\subsection{Visibility Clique Cover}
\label{sec:vcc}

Visibility Clique Cover (VCC) constructs a cover from the global visibility
structure of free space~\cite{DeitsTedrake2023VCC}.  It samples collision-free
configurations and connects two samples when their straight-line segment remains
collision free.  A clique identifies mutually visible samples that initialize a
collision-free seed region for inflation.  Starting from an ellipsoidal seed,
an IRIS-style region-inflation procedure alternates between computing
obstacle-separating hyperplanes and expanding a maximum-volume ellipsoid inside
their polytope intersection.  The converged polytope is a convex,
collision-free region~\cite{DeitsTedrake2014}.

The resulting polytopes can be larger than local ACD pieces and may overlap.
Convexity gives the optimizer freedom to place a B\'ezier segment anywhere
within a region, while an overlap supplies a nonempty connection set for
adjacent segments.  This can reduce the number of active regions and alter the
shape of the optimized path.  However, extensive overlap can also create many
candidate GCS transitions, so a compact cover does not guarantee a sparse
region graph.  The total construction cost includes sampling and visibility
tests as well as repeated region-inflation calls; it can increase with the sampling
budget and coverage target.  Sparse or unrepresentative samples can make narrow
passages difficult to cover or connect.
